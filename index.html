The Story of the Bean: Management and Entrepreneurship Lens

Examine how Copper Cup could turn a proposed coffee pilot into organized, ethical, and adaptable work.

Case Context

Copper Cup is preparing a limited operational test for The Story of the Bean. For this scenario, the draft plan uses a one-year direct cooperative agreement and modest traceability tools. Before making a longer-term commitment, owner Elena Torres wants to learn whether the team can deliver the product accurately, responsibly, and consistently during a busy cafe launch.

The proposal looks promising on paper. Now Copper Cup has to determine how people, roles, goals, and operating systems would make it work.

Launch Signal 1: The Morning Rush

Customers want the story, but the line slows when employees try to explain the sourcing relationship. Some employees shorten the message; others add claims that Copper Cup has not verified.

Launch Signal 2: A Difficult Goal

The launch dashboard calls for 420 featured drinks per week. Employees worry that a sales-only target will pressure them to oversell the product and ignore customer fit, workload, or evidence quality.

Elena asks Maya a new question: "How do we keep the promise to the cooperative, customers, and employees when the work is more complicated than the plan?"

Management and entrepreneurship decision: How should Copper Cup organize the launch, lead employees, control quality, and learn from the pilot without turning purpose into pressure?

Management and Entrepreneurship: When the Bean Becomes Work

Management and Entrepreneurship asks how people turn opportunity into organized action. The opportunity is clear: Copper Cup can create a coffee experience that helps customers understand the people and systems behind the bean. But ideas do not execute themselves. Someone has to plan the launch, organize roles, lead employees, measure results, and adapt when reality is messier than the plan.

Management begins with organizations. Organizations divide work among people with different skills so they can accomplish goals that would be difficult to achieve alone. That efficiency also creates challenges: people need motivation and commitment, teams need coordination, leaders need judgment, and the organization needs a strategy that connects daily work to competitive advantage.

The management discipline examines both the micro and macro sides of those challenges. The micro or organizational-behavior side studies how individuals and teams perform, communicate, negotiate, lead, respond to conflict, and remain committed. The macro or strategic side asks how a firm builds capabilities, positions itself against competitors, allocates resources, and creates value that is difficult to imitate.

Entrepreneurship is not limited to opening a company. It is a way of finding an opportunity, forming a value proposition, testing assumptions, assembling resources, and adjusting when evidence challenges the original idea. A student can use that mindset in a startup, a family business, a nonprofit, or an established company that needs a new product or process.

Human resources makes the people system visible. Copper Cup needs to recruit, select, onboard, train, schedule, support, evaluate, and retain employees. It also needs employment practices that are consistent, job-related, respectful, and compliant with applicable law. The product promise cannot survive if the company treats people as interchangeable labor.

Elena writes a launch goal:

In eight weeks, Copper Cup will introduce The Story of the Bean in a way that increases customer understanding, protects employee workload, and meets the sales level needed to sustain the cooperative relationship.

Maya sees the four functions of management. Planning defines goals, assumptions, timelines, and decisions. Organizing assigns work, authority, information, and resources. Leading influences people to understand the purpose, contribute ideas, and perform with confidence. Controlling compares actual results with standards so managers can communicate deviations, correct problems, and reinforce what works.

The entrepreneurship lens adds a testable assumption: customers will care enough about the bean's story to support a more transparent sourcing model. Copper Cup does not yet know whether the right format is a premium latte, a small drip, a retail bag, a QR page, a short employee script, or some combination. The pilot should create evidence before the company scales.

The first week reveals the human side of the launch. During the morning rush, customers ask detailed questions. Some employees are excited. Others feel they are being asked to sell a more expensive drink while the line grows longer. Jordan, the shift lead, tells Maya, "I like the purpose, but if the goal is just to sell more, people will start saying whatever gets the order finished."

That warning is supported by management research in the course content. Difficult goals can motivate performance, but goals that feel unattainable or threatening can increase uncertainty, anxiety, burnout, and unethical behavior. Employees may narrow their attention to the measured target and stop helping coworkers. Involving employees in goal setting and providing support can increase enthusiasm and preserve the citizenship behaviors that keep a team functioning.

Elena revises the plan. Instead of requiring an upsell to every customer, employees ask one question: "Would you like to try the new roast and hear where it came from?" During rush periods, they give a short evidence-based explanation and point interested customers to the QR page. During slower periods, they can tell the fuller story.

The team also creates an ethical voice channel. Priya uses it immediately: "The sign says this coffee supports farmers. Can we say exactly how?" The team examines the evidence and changes the language:

Copper Cup pays a premium through a direct cooperative relationship and is piloting traceability tools to show more of the bean's journey.

The new statement is less dramatic and more defensible. Research on ethical voice shows why this matters. Formal policies are not enough. Employees need practical ways to raise a complaint, suggest a more ethical action, or ask a question that redirects attention to an ethical issue. Leaders must respond without punishing the person who speaks.

Copper Cup's management problem is therefore not choosing between performance and responsibility. Effective leaders recognize that some tensions require a both-and response: sales and accuracy, customer access and financial sustainability, individual performance and teamwork, strategic control and employee voice.

Management and Entrepreneurship shows that responsibility is not merely an intention. It is a practice that has to be planned, organized, led, measured, corrected, and learned.

Four Functions in Action

The functions overlap in real work. A manager may plan a training standard, organize who teaches it, lead the team through practice, and control the process by checking whether employees can use the script accurately during a rush.

1

Planning

Set goals, assumptions, timelines, standards, and alternatives before committing resources.

2

Organizing

Assign roles, authority, information, schedules, and resources so work can move.

3

Leading

Influence, communicate, coach, listen, and build commitment to shared goals.

4

Controlling

Compare results with standards, communicate gaps, correct problems, and reinforce success.

Customer claim Elena and Priya Menu, QR page, and employee script use the same evidence boundary. Pause or revise language when a claim exceeds available evidence.

Inventory and waste Opening manager Batch size reflects forecast demand and available cooperative coffee. Adjust the next batch when stockouts or closing waste exceed the standard.
Pilot learning Maya Sales, questions, QR scans, workload, and evidence status are reviewed weekly. Recommend a pivot when the data contradict the launch assumptions.

The Five-Step Control Process

  1. Set standards.
    Define the expected result and acceptable range.
  2. Monitor results.
    Collect relevant, timely, and usable evidence.
  3. Compare.
    Identify gaps between actual results and standards.
  4. Communicate.
    Share results and deviations with the people involved.
  5. Act and learn.
    Correct problems and reinforce successful performance.

AI at Work: Reviewing the Control Board

AI could summarize shift notes or flag patterns in sales, wait time, and customer questions. A manager still decides whether the measures are comparable, whether the data omit employee experience, and whether a correction solves the cause instead of merely changing the number.

People, Leadership, and Human Resources

Managers coordinate goals, resources, roles, and controls. Leaders influence direction, meaning, trust, and commitment. The same person may do both, but a detailed schedule does not automatically inspire people, and a compelling vision does not replace staffing or follow-through.

Leadership style should fit the situation. A directive approach may be appropriate for immediate safety or compliance requirements. A participative approach uses employee knowledge when redesigning the rush workflow. Coaching builds skill through practice and feedback. Transformational or purpose-centered leadership connects daily work to a larger reason while still respecting evidence and workload.

Human resource management helps Copper Cup attract, select, onboard, train, compensate, support, and retain the people who make the launch possible. HR also connects people decisions to business strategy. The company cannot create a differentiated customer experience without employees who have the knowledge, time, motivation, and authority to deliver it.

Artifact: Copper Cup's Six-Step Selection Plan

Step Copper Cup Action Management Reason
1. Define criteria Identify job-related service, learning, teamwork, and communication requirements. Selection begins with the work, not a manager's personal similarity preference.
2. Recruit applicants Describe duties, schedule, pay range, and essential requirements accurately. A realistic preview improves fit and reduces avoidable turnover.
3. Screen consistently Review applications against the same documented criteria. Consistency improves decision quality and supports fair treatment.
4. Interview and sample Ask structured questions and use a short, accessible customer-scenario exercise. Job-related evidence is stronger than an informal "gut feeling."
5. Verify lawfully Check relevant references or background information consistently and with notice. Screening should be necessary, proportionate, documented, and privacy-conscious.
6. Offer and onboard Make the offer, complete lawful post-offer steps, train, and provide early feedback. Selection continues through preparation, support, and transfer of training to the job.

How Legislation Shapes HR Decisions

  • Equal opportunity and nondiscrimination: Criteria and decisions must be job-related and applied consistently.
  • Accessibility and accommodation: Applicants and employees may need reasonable access to selection, training, scheduling, and work.
  • Wage, hour, leave, and scheduling requirements: Labor budgets and launch expectations must reflect lawful paid work and applicable protections.
  • Safety and respectful work: Managers need prevention, reporting, response, and documentation practices.
  • Privacy and records: Applicant and employee information should be collected only for a legitimate purpose and handled carefully.

These principles are instructional examples, not a complete legal checklist. Copper Cup would need current professional guidance for the laws that apply to its location and workforce.

Goal Design and Citizenship

A difficult goal should include employee input when possible, the resources needed to perform, clear ethical boundaries, and measures beyond sales. That design can reduce uncertainty and burnout while protecting the helping, mentoring, and teamwork behaviors that formal job descriptions often miss.

Research That Changes Practice

  • Ethical voice: Ned Wellman's research shows how complaints, suggestions, and ethical questions can help employees redirect questionable behavior.
  • Goal setting: Mike Baer and David Welsh examine how imposed or unattainable goals can increase anxiety, burnout, reduced citizenship, and unethical choices.
  • Leadership and responsibility: David Waldman studies socially responsible leadership, paradoxes, technology transfer, and how leaders mobilize people around goals.
  • Identity and stigma: Blake Ashforth and Milo Wang examine how people and organizations experience and manage stigmatized work or identities.
  • Difference and visibility: Heewon Chae and Jonathan Bundy study atypical organizations, evaluation, strategic nonconformity, and the benefits and burdens of CEO celebrity.

Management and Entrepreneurship Decision Point

Copper Cup's first week creates employee stress and customer confusion. Elena must adjust the launch without abandoning the cooperative promise or rewarding unsupported claims.

Artifact: Employee Message Thread

  • Jordan, shift leadMorning rush is my concern. We cannot explain a sourcing model while ten people are waiting unless we have a short version.
  • Priya, baristaCan we get exact wording for what "supports farmers" means? I do not want to overpromise.
  • Maya, student employeeMaybe we use one question at the register and send interested customers to the QR card for the full story.
  • Sam, baristaWhat if someone asks whether it is fair trade?
  • Elena, ownerSay: "This is a direct cooperative pilot, not a fair trade certified coffee. Here is what we verify and what we are still learning."

Option A: Push Harder on the Sales Goal

Business upside: May increase short-term revenue and move inventory quickly.

Business risk: Can increase burnout, exaggerated claims, narrow attention, and weaker teamwork.

Social question: When does a performance goal become harmful?

Option B: Slow the Launch and Retrain

Business upside: Improves employee confidence, accuracy, and consistent customer communication.

Business risk: Reduces immediate momentum and may delay learning from live customer behavior.

Social question: Is slower execution justified when the product story involves social responsibility?

Option C: Keep the Pilot Active and Simplify Expectations

Business upside: Protects momentum while using a short script, clear claim boundary, employee voice, and weekly review.

Business risk: Some customers may learn less unless they choose to engage with the fuller story.

Social question: How can Copper Cup respect workload and customers' right to accurate information?
