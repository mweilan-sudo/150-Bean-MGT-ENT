Case Context

Copper Cup Coffee is a small independent cafe considering a 12-week Story of the Bean pilot. The concept would help customers follow a featured coffee from farm and cooperative to roaster and cup. No other department's decision is assumed in this module. The management team must decide for itself whether the work is ready, who should perform it, and how the cafe will learn from the trial.

A customer orders the featured coffee and notices a small card beside the register: "Meet the people behind this week's coffee." The card identifies the cooperative and roaster using information Copper Cup has checked. Customers can read it or ask a question. Employees are ready to answer briefly; they are not expected to give a presentation with every purchase.

Owner Elena Torres has identified a possible part-time assignment: Traceability and Customer Education Coordinator, called the pilot coordinator here. Traceability means being able to follow the coffee through records from its origin to the cafe. The coordinator would check those records with partners, maintain an approved coffee information sheet, coach employees, and report what the pilot is teaching the company. Maya Alvarez, a student employee helping coordinate the pilot, is preparing options for Elena's approval.

The information sheet is a short internal reference: what employees may say, which records support it, what remains unverified, and whom to ask for help. The supporting invoices, shipment records, and partner documents stay in a separate records folder. The customer-facing origin card contains only a few approved details, not the internal sheet or its supporting records.

What is a public claim? It is a statement Copper Cup makes to customers, whether spoken by an employee or written on a sign, origin card, or website. Here, we call these customer-facing statements. Partners provide information and evidence; Copper Cup remains responsible for what it tells customers.

Copper Cup is still a small cafe. Its cooperative, importing partner, and roaster supply records; a scheduling vendor offers an AI feature; an outside HR adviser can help with employment questions. Copper Cup does not need its own technology or HR department to face management decisions. The people, events, and pilot figures in this scenario are fictional.

Operating Signal 1: Inconsistent Customer Information

Employees are enthusiastic, but their explanations vary. One says the coffee is “direct trade.” Another says “farmers receive every premium dollar.” Copper Cup does not yet have evidence supporting either statement.

Operating Signal 2: A Staffing Shortcut

A manager suggests bringing in an unpaid student intern to maintain the approved coffee information sheet and supporting records folder, cover two weekly rush shifts, and replace the seasonal employee Copper Cup had planned to hire.

Your management question: Who should carry the bean's story, how should Copper Cup support that person, and what should people keep control of when AI can help?

Operating Signal 3: An AI Shortcut

The scheduling vendor offers to recommend shifts or publish them automatically. Jordan, a shift lead, asks, "Will it know when someone needs coaching, or when a student has an exam?" Elena needs to choose the system's authority, not just turn it on.

Management and Entrepreneurship: Who Will Carry the Story?

On Monday morning, Elena places a green coffee sample, a shipping record, and a draft cafe sign on a table near the counter. The sign says, "Farmers receive every premium dollar." The shipping record identifies a coffee shipment; it does not show how money reached individual farmers. Elena sets the sign aside until that statement can be verified.

“The idea is good,” Elena tells Maya. “But who will answer a customer's question when the line reaches the door?” Maya has worked that rush. She knows a good story can become a difficult shift when nobody knows who checks the facts or trains the team.

They picture an ordinary order: a customer buys the featured coffee, notices the optional origin card, and asks where the beans came from. The barista gives one brief answer from the approved coffee information sheet. If the customer asks how much a farmer received, the barista does not guess: "I don't have verified information about that payment. I can pass your question to our coordinator." The barista continues serving rather than searching supplier records during the rush.

Management is the process of accomplishing organizational goals through planning, organizing, leading, and controlling people and other resources. Entrepreneurship accepts the risk of starting and running a business, but entrepreneurial thinking also appears inside an existing organization whenever people identify an opportunity, test assumptions, and adjust with evidence. Copper Cup needs both disciplines: a worthwhile opportunity and a workable organization.

A Small Test Before a Bigger Commitment

Maya begins with planning. Copper Cup's broad, long-range aim is to become known for credible and understandable sourcing information. For Elena's approval, she translates that strategy into a 12-week pilot: one featured coffee, one approved coffee information sheet, an optional customer origin card, and a weekly records review. She drafts work standards and schedules, then asks what the team will do if documentation is missing. Strategic, tactical, operational, and contingency planning answer different questions, but they must reinforce one another.

The first two weeks will test an assumption: can interested customers learn about the coffee from the optional card and brief answers without overwhelming employees? A value proposition is the benefit a business offers customers. Here, it is a good cup of coffee with a clear, credible account of its origin. Elena is not assuming customers value that benefit just because she does. Maya proposes making the card available on a few comparable shifts, answering questions when customers ask, inviting willing customers to share what they learned, and asking employees what slowed them down. Customers can simply buy their coffee and leave.

Hiring Is a Two-Way Decision

Next comes organizing. The cooperative, Northstar Coffee Importers, and Sonoran Roasting Company supply records about their part of the coffee's journey. The coordinator checks that those records describe the featured coffee, prepares the information sheet, and coaches employees. Elena approves customer-facing statements before they appear on the card, a sign, the website, or in employee answers. Employees use the sheet and refer unanswered questions to the coordinator.

If support for a statement is missing or changes, the coordinator pauses its use and alerts Elena; Elena approves any replacement before it is shared. Employees flag concerns rather than filling gaps themselves. Organizing is not merely drawing boxes. It allocates resources, assigns tasks, establishes procedures, and places people where they can contribute most effectively.

The staffing conversation exposes a problem. Jordan, a shift lead, could grow into the role with training. An external applicant might bring stronger data experience. A student internship could add a genuine learning partnership. Yet the proposed unpaid internship is designed mainly to cover productive work and replace a planned employee. Maya flags the idea for careful review rather than treating the word “intern” as a wage exemption.

"We need the right person, not just someone available," Elena says. Maya drafts a realistic description of the hours, pay, coaching duties, and record work. Candidates need to judge whether the job works for them, too. Copper Cup will use the same core interview questions and a short sample record-checking task. Elena and the HR adviser will arrange any appropriate checks at the lawful stage; the cafe will not copy an old hiring checklist without checking its requirements.

A signed offer is not the finish line. Onboarding means helping a new person learn the work and become part of the team. Maya proposes a first-day introduction to partners and evidence rules, a coached practice conversation, and a week-one check-in. If the new coordinator struggles, Elena must ask whether expectations, training, or workload need to change before blaming the person.

Support People Throughout the Work

Leading begins before anyone is hired. Employees need to understand why accurate customer information matters, what authority the coordinator will hold, and how concerns can be raised. The leadership approach should fit the person and the work. A new employee may need close guidance while learning legal and evidence boundaries. An experienced coordinator may be given freedom to design the weekly workflow within clear limits.

Controlling closes the loop. Maya proposes five steps: establish clear standards, monitor and record performance, compare results against standards, communicate results, and take corrective action when needed. The final action can also reveal that the original standard was unrealistic. Control is therefore not a hunt for blame; it is a feedback process.

Priya, a barista, worries that a sales target will reward whoever makes the boldest promise about the coffee. Elena thanks her for speaking up and keeps accuracy and workload in the weekly review. Employees are not rewarded for giving a speech to every customer. This is ethical voice: raising a concern about what is right, even when the concern is inconvenient. A demanding goal can motivate effort, but an unrealistic one can encourage shortcuts.

Human resources reaches beyond the hire. Honest recruitment helps attract people; coaching helps them develop; fair treatment and workable schedules can help retain them. Even a departing employee matters. A respectful handoff may leave that person willing to recommend Copper Cup to a friend. That willingness to recommend the workplace is employee advocacy, not something the cafe can demand.

The new role will require technical skill with records and simple data tools, conceptual skill to see how sourcing evidence affects operations and reputation, and human-relations skill to coach employees and work across organizations. The coordinator will also perform interpersonal, informational, and decisional roles: build relationships, gather and share reliable information, and act when evidence or workflow breaks down.

Decide What to Delegate

The vendor's AI can draft a schedule, recommend one, or take action within rules. These are different levels of authority. For the pilot, Elena chooses advice: AI may suggest coverage using approved availability and role requirements, but it cannot publish shifts, rank applicants, or approve customer-facing statements about the coffee. Maya and Jordan review the draft for training needs and workload, employees can flag errors, and Elena approves it.

"If the schedule is unfair, we cannot say the software did it," Elena says. A faster draft does not transfer accountability. The same principle applies to the coffee's story: AI can organize approved records, but a confident summary is not evidence that a farmer received a particular benefit.

Elena asks for one staffing choice, one first-week support step, and one small test of the customer explanation. Maya does not need a perfect business plan. She needs a practical next step that the team can try, review, and improve.

AI at Work: Useful Drafts, Human Accountability

AI could draft a role description, map responsibilities, generate interview-question ideas, or summarize weekly operating data. Managers remain responsible for verifying job relevance, protecting applicant and employee data, checking for bias and accessibility barriers, applying current law, and making the final decision. An AI ranking is not neutral simply because it is automated.

The Four Functions in Action

The functions overlap. Maya can move among them in a single meeting, but each function answers a different management question.

1

Planning

Set goals, choose strategies, identify resources, define standards, and prepare alternatives.

2

Organizing

Allocate resources, assign tasks, establish authority, recruit people, and create procedures.

3

Leading

Create a vision, motivate people, clarify routines and policies, and provide feedback.

4

Controlling

Measure performance against standards, communicate results, reward success, and correct problems.

The Five-Step Control Process

  1. Establish clear standards.
    Define the expected result and acceptable evidence.
  2. Monitor and record.
    Collect timely performance information.
  3. Compare results.
    Evaluate actual performance against standards.
  4. Communicate results.
    Share what is working and what differs.
  5. Take corrective action.
    Fix performance or revise unrealistic standards.

Control Artifact: Pilot Standards

  • Every customer-facing statement comes from the approved coffee information sheet and has a matching supporting record.
  • Documentation gaps are recorded within one business day.
  • Employees answer briefly when asked, offer the optional card, and refer unknowns without inventing details.
  • Weekly reports include evidence quality, workload, questions, and service impact.

What Managers Do

Management level changes the mix of skills a person uses. First-line managers often rely heavily on technical skill. Top managers need more conceptual skill. Human-relations skill matters at every level.

Technical Skill

Perform and understand a specialized task. The coordinator must match records to the featured coffee and maintain the approved coffee information sheet.

Conceptual Skill

See the organization as a whole. The coordinator must connect evidence choices to staffing, service, reputation, and strategy.

Human-Relations Skill

Work effectively with people. The coordinator must coach employees, listen to concerns, and collaborate across organizations.

Three Managerial Roles

Interpersonal

Build relationships and connect people. Maya introduces the coordinator to shift leads, partners, and decision makers.

Informational

Acquire and deliver accurate information. The coordinator gathers documentation and translates it for employees and customers.

Decisional

Use information to take action. The coordinator pauses unsupported language and recommends a correction or new test.

Managers and Leaders

Management coordinates goals, resources, roles, and performance. Leadership creates direction and influences people to pursue it. A formal manager needs both, while an employee can provide leadership without holding a management title.

Leadership ranges from manager-centered to employee-centered. A manager may make and announce a decision when a legal or safety boundary is not negotiable. The same manager may ask a skilled employee to design a workflow within clearly defined limits. Effective leadership considers the employee's expertise and motivation, the risk of the task, and the amount of discretion the situation permits.

How Management Is Changing

  • Flatter structures: Fewer management layers and more self-managed teams move judgment closer to the work.
  • Distributed collaboration: Managers maintain relationships and reliable information across in-person, remote, and partner networks.
  • Contingent work: Employees, contractors, gig workers, and interns create new flexibility alongside classification, coordination, and fairness questions.
  • AI-supported decisions: Faster analysis can assist managers, but privacy, bias, accessibility, source quality, and accountability remain human responsibilities.

AI at Work: Supporting the Informational Role

AI can summarize notes, flag inconsistent wording, or organize weekly signals. It cannot establish trust, understand every context, or accept accountability. Maya should verify source records, let employees correct the summary, and keep consequential decisions with responsible people.

People and AI at Work

AI can support all four management functions. It might compare pilot scenarios for planning, draft shift coverage for organizing, suggest a coaching message for leading, or flag missing records for controlling. People supply context, check the evidence, and remain accountable for decisions.

How Much Authority?

Automation performs a task, such as formatting a schedule. Augmentation combines human judgment with AI support, such as comparing a suggested schedule with employee needs. Agency lets AI take actions toward a goal within boundaries. These describe ways of working, not a requirement to give AI more power.

  • People must: approve schedules, make hiring decisions, coach employees, and resolve concerns. Elena approves customer-facing coffee statements after the coordinator checks the records.

People, Selection, and Employment Law

Human resource management helps an organization attract, select, onboard, develop, support, compensate, and retain people. Employment law affects the entire employee life cycle. Coverage and requirements can depend on the law, employer size, location, worker status, and specific facts, so managers should use current agency guidance and qualified advice.

Legal Boundaries in This Case

  • Nondiscrimination: Recruitment, interviews, tests, assignments, pay, and promotion should use job-related criteria and avoid unlawful discrimination.
  • Accessibility: Applicants and employees may need reasonable accommodation, including access to the application and assessment process.
  • Wage and hour: Calling a person an intern does not by itself determine whether that person is an employee entitled to minimum wage and overtime protections.
  • Work authorization and safety: Hiring includes lawful identity and work-authorization procedures, while managers must also train people for workplace hazards.
  • Changing requirements: Federal, state, and local rules evolve. Managers need reliable sources and should not treat an old checklist or AI answer as current legal advice.

From Recruiting to Onboarding

Hiring is a two-way decision: Copper Cup assesses job-related capabilities, and candidates assess the work, expectations, and support. Managers can recruit internally or externally. A clear role description and an easy, accessible application help people decide whether to apply.

  1. Define and recruit.
    Explain the duties, pay, hours, and support; choose where to reach suitable candidates.
  2. Application and screening.
    Collect relevant information through an accessible process and apply consistent criteria.
  3. Interview and work sample.
    Ask common core questions, explain the real work, and assess a relevant task.
  4. Offer and appropriate checks.
    Use a conditional offer where appropriate and arrange relevant checks at the lawful stage.
  5. Prepare and welcome.
    Confirm requirements, arrange access and training, and introduce the new person to the team.
  6. Coach and follow up.
    Practice the work together, ask what is unclear, and adjust support during the first weeks.

Timing matters: This is Copper Cup's planning workflow, not a universal legal sequence. The timing and scope of checks depend on the check, location, and applicable law. Under the ADA, covered employers cannot require pre-offer medical exams; post-offer exams have conditions too. Consult EEOC medical-inquiry guidance and qualified advice rather than applying one rule to every kind of check.

The Employee Experience Continues

HR balances business goals with people's needs across their time with an organization. Copper Cup's employer brand, or reputation as a workplace, should match what the job is actually like. Recruitment and selection lead into onboarding, development, and retention. When someone leaves, a respectful transition can support advocacy: they may recommend the cafe to others or return later.

For example, a coordinator who keeps missing record reviews may need training, clearer priorities, or protected time. Replacing the person without examining the work design may recreate the same problem.

Artifact: Is This Really an Unpaid Internship?

For a for-profit employer, the U.S. Department of Labor uses a flexible “primary beneficiary” test to examine the economic reality of an unpaid internship. No single factor decides the result. Relevant questions include whether there is an expectation of compensation; whether training resembles an educational environment; whether the experience is tied to formal education; whether it accommodates academic commitments; whether its duration is limited to beneficial learning; whether the intern complements rather than displaces paid employees while receiving significant educational benefits; and whether both sides understand there is no promise of a paid job afterward.

Research That Changes Practice

  • Goal setting: Difficult goals can energize performance, but threatening or unrealistic goals can narrow attention, increase strain, and weaken helping behavior.
  • Ethical voice: Employees need practical ways to question, suggest, or challenge actions, and leaders must respond without retaliation.
  • Leadership: Responsible leaders balance stakeholder needs and adapt their approach to the people, work, and uncertainty involved.
  • Identity and stigma: Research on dirty work and organizational stigma examines how people maintain dignity and meaning when society devalues their work.
  • Organizations and reputation: Studies of atypical organizations and celebrity chief executives show how identity and visibility can affect strategy and stakeholder judgment.

Three Staffing Paths

  • Internal developmentGive a current employee a defined assignment, training, authority, and compensation. This preserves organization knowledge but may create coverage and development needs elsewhere.
  • External employeeHire a part-time employee through a structured process. This can add specialized experience but requires sourcing, selection, onboarding, and integration.

Artifact: A Two-Week Learning Test

Inside the 12-week pilot, Copper Cup will make the optional origin card available on a few comparable shifts. Employees will answer questions briefly from the approved coffee information sheet, not deliver a presentation with every purchase. The hypothesis is that interested customers can understand the origin better without creating unmanageable work.

  • Observe: Record recurring questions and referrals, invite willing customers who read the card or asked a question to explain what they learned, and note service delays and employee concerns. Customers may decline.
  • Compare: Review similar shifts and record differences in staffing or customer traffic. A small trial offers clues, not proof that the explanation caused every result.
  • Adjust: If customers confuse origin with certification, ask Elena to approve clearer wording. If service slows, shorten answers or arrange follow-up for complex questions. If neither helps, pause and rethink the offer.

This is entrepreneurial learning: test an assumption with a manageable commitment, listen, and decide what to try next. Positive comments alone are not a reason to expand.
